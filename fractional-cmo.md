A **fractional CMO** is a senior marketing executive who works part-time for your company, usually 10 to 20 hours a week on a monthly retainer, and owns marketing the way a full-time chief marketing officer would: strategy, budget, team, vendors, and results. Companies hire one when they need executive marketing leadership but don't need, or can't yet justify, a full-time CMO.

That's the textbook answer. Here's what it leaves out: a fractional CMO is only as good as the playbook they bring. Plenty are still running the inbound, MQL-driven model that quit working when B2B buyers moved their research into AI assistants, peer communities, and private channels. Hire one of those part-time and you get the old playbook at a discount.

I've done this job full-time, as CMO at Aerospike and SVP of Marketing at EDB, and now I do it fractionally at [Tangyslice Collective](/about/), the two-person fractional CMO and AI-native marketing consultancy Tim Dailey and I run in Massachusetts. This guide covers what the role involves, how it compares to an agency, a consultant, or a full-time hire, what it costs, and when it's the right call.

## What does a fractional CMO do?

A fractional CMO sets marketing strategy, owns the marketing budget and plan, leads the people and vendors who execute it, and reports results to the CEO and board. What separates the role from an advisor is accountability. When pipeline misses, the fractional CMO is the one explaining why in the board meeting.

Core fractional CMO responsibilities:

1. **Strategy and positioning.** Define who you sell to, why they buy, and what you can say that competitors can't. Everything else depends on this.
2. **Plan and budget.** Turn strategy into a quarterly plan with a budget, channel mix, and targets the CFO will sign off on.
3. **Team leadership.** Manage, coach, and hire the marketing team. Decide what to build in-house and what to contract.
4. **Vendor and agency management.** Hold agencies, freelancers, and software vendors to outcomes. The ones that can only report activity go.
5. **Measurement and reporting.** Pick the handful of metrics that predict revenue and report them honestly to leadership and the board.
6. **Sales alignment.** Agree with sales on what counts as a real opportunity, so the two teams stop arguing about lead quality.
7. **Go-to-market for launches.** Own the market side of product launches, pricing changes, and entry into new segments.

### What is a fractional CMO agency?

A fractional CMO agency, or firm, supplies fractional CMOs instead of a single independent executive. Some are marketplaces that match you with a vetted freelancer. Others are small partnerships where the founders do the work themselves, which is how Tangyslice works.

Firms can bring a bench of skills that one person can't. They also bring the oldest move in the agency business: a senior partner pitches the deal, and a mid-level account manager runs your Tuesday standups. Before you sign, ask exactly who shows up each week and get the name in the contract. We don't have account managers between clients and the work, because there are only two of us.

## How much does a fractional CMO cost?

A fractional CMO typically costs **$3,000 to $20,000 a month** on retainer, or **$150 to $500 an hour**. Those are combined ranges from two 2026 pricing guides: MarketerHire puts most retainers at $3,000 to $15,000 a month, and GoFractional at $4,000 to $20,000. A full-time CMO runs about $260,000 to $520,000 or more in the first year once salary of $175,000 to $300,000+, benefits, equity, and recruiting fees are added up, according to MarketerHire.

The retainer is rarely the whole bill. Budget separately for the programs the CMO runs: paid media, content, tools, and any agencies they keep. A good fractional CMO should be cutting some of that spend within the first quarter.

## When should you hire a fractional CMO?

Hire a fractional CMO when marketing needs an owner and a full-time executive would be too much, too soon, or too slow to find. The common signs:

- You don't have a head of marketing, and the CEO is still making every marketing call between sales meetings.
- You have marketers executing without a clear strategy. Activity is up. Pipeline isn't.
- Pipeline slowed and nobody can explain what changed, even though spend stayed the same.
- You're paying agencies and tools you can't evaluate, because nobody senior owns them.
- You're entering a new market or launching a product and need a credible marketing plan fast.
- You plan to hire a full-time CMO in a year or two and want the foundation, team, and metrics in place first.

When it's the wrong call: if the founders haven't yet figured out how to close their first ten customers themselves, a fractional CMO just gives you an expensive person to organize a message that doesn't convert yet. Sell it yourself first. And if you're running large teams through enterprise sales cycles and procurement, you probably need a full-time CMO, and you're outside who we serve anyway.

## What changes when buyers research with AI?

Your buyers now ask ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude, and Google's AI Overviews which vendors to consider, long before they visit your site or fill in a form. If you're missing from that answer, you're missing from the shortlist. None of it shows up in your analytics.

That changes the fractional CMO job in three ways.

1. **Visibility in AI answers becomes a pipeline metric.** A modern fractional CMO checks what AI assistants say about your company and your category, then fixes the gaps with structured content, entity building, and mentions on the sites those assistants trust. We call this [AI visibility and generative search optimization](/services/#visibility).
2. **The MQL loses its job as the scoreboard.** Buyers who arrive already decided don't download three ebooks first. We explain why in [The MQL Was Always a Fiction](/blog/why-mqls-dont-work/).
3. **A fractional CMO can build the tools a lean team can't afford to buy.** Last year I needed an attribution model. The tools I'd used before cost around $30,000 a year. I built one in a Google Sheet with Claude in about two hours, and the board was happy with it. It worked because I knew the logic, and the AI handled the assembly. That's the pattern behind the [AI workflows we build](/services/#systems): they live in your stack, run on your data, and stay with you.

My rule for when to build instead of buy: the workflow is narrow, the data and APIs are cheap to reach, and customization is worth more than the upkeep. If any one of those fails, buy.

So the bar has moved. In 2026 a fractional CMO has to get you into the AI answer, and the Google ranking is now only half the job.
